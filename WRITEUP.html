Qalaisyn: making AI understand Kazakh the way people actually type

Track: Languages · Team: Zhasulan B.

The problem

Most phones in Kazakhstan have a Russian keyboard, so people drop the Kazakh-only letters (ә ғ қ ң ө ұ ү һ і) and type калайсын instead of қалайсың; others text in Latin (qalaisyn). Translators and chatbots are trained on standard Kazakh. We measured the cost on held-out data: on a Russian keyboard almost every second word (47%) is misspelled and a translator loses about a quarter of the meaning; in Latin it loses 75%. As banks, clinics and e-government services put AI first in line, people who type "the way their phone lets them" get worse help. The same happens in other languages whose letters get dropped on phones (Uzbek, Kyrgyz, Azerbaijani, Vietnamese…).

What we built

Qalaisyn is not an app people download. It is a small AI layer that sits inside any chatbot, translator or keyboard, like autocorrect. In: баламнын кызуы 39 котерилди не истеу керек. Out: баламның қызуы 39 көтерілді не істеу керек ("my child's temperature rose to 39, what should I do"), which the AI now understands. When unsure, it shows suggestions (ot → от 73% · өт 27%) instead of silently guessing. User: Gulnar, 62, from a village in Turkistan region, texting a clinic chatbot at night from a phone with only a Russian keyboard. Where it is used every day: bank, airline and delivery support chats, clinic bots, e-government. Live example: an airline bot's translator reads ушак кешигип жатыр ма as "is there a rumor going on?"; after Qalaisyn, "is the plane running late?". A bank bot reads кредит толей алмай журмин комектесиниздер as "non-credited commetticines"; after Qalaisyn, "I can't pay my loan, please help".

What we added

Our own idea (not a suggested project). Our own data and questions: (1) a generator that simulates six real typing styles (Russian keyboard, partial Kazakh keyboard, four Latin styles), so we never need anyone's private chats; (2) stress tests that ask when does it break?; (3) calibrated confidence and suggestions; (4) a transfer test on Kyrgyz.

How it works

Data: 1.5M Kazakh Wikipedia + 4k Tatoeba sentences for training. Held-out tests: 623 everyday Tatoeba and FLORES-200 news sentences, each with a professional English translation. Tuning on FLORES dev only; no test sentence was seen in training. Model: For each typed word we list the Kazakh words it could be (к → к/қ, а → а/ә, и → и/і …). A typing-habit model and a word-bigram language model score them, and Viterbi decoding picks the most likely sentence. Unknown words go to a character 5-gram model, likely typos to a one-edit neighbour search. Forward-backward gives each word a probability, and words under 90% become suggestions. It runs in milliseconds on a laptop.

Results (held-out test data; translator NLLB-200, chrF vs. professional English)

Russian keyboard + 15% typos: chrF 60.8 36.0 52.6 57.6 (87% vs. 67%)
Latin chat: translation chrF 60.8 15.3 58.4 59.9 (98% vs. 95%)
Latin, unseen spellings (ae, kk): chrF 60.8 14.2 39.3 54.4 (86% vs. 54%)

Suggestions: only 5% of words are flagged, yet they contain 59% of all Qalaisyn's mistakes, and for 75% of those the right word is among the suggestions. Words it is sure about are wrong only 1.4% of the time. Transfer: the same code trained on Kyrgyz Wikipedia recovers 97% of the lost meaning for Kyrgyz. Honest notes: on clean text a simple dictionary is close; Qalaisyn's advantage is on real, messy messages and in knowing when it is unsure. Where it fails: н/ң and і/и when both words exist, and true ambiguity (ot = от "fire" / өт "pass"). Some "errors" are typos in the reference (қагаз, сатыл ал) that Qalaisyn fixed.

What we'd do next

  1. Longer context (trigrams or a small neural re-ranker) for н/ң and і/и. 2. A keyboard and a plug-in API for public-service chatbots; more languages (Uzbek, Azerbaijani).
